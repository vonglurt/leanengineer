Abstract--The author's statement of the topic is: Lean allows proofs
using real numbers, yet not [proofs about] floating-point errors.
The lecture proposes to bridge the two with one polymorphic function,
proved at the real numbers and executed at floating-point numbers.
This note records the claim and its assumptions, poses the opening
questions, and gives a preliminary analysis. The analysis separates
three layers -- the theory is correct, the code is the theory, the
code is numerically close -- and finds that the bridge secures the
second layer and leaves the third unproved. For the reader of DEC-00
the third layer is tolerance analysis, and the open problem is to
state it as a theorem.

Index Terms--floating point, real numbers, round-off error,
polymorphism, error analysis, quantization, Lean 4.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

The slide on screen at 1:08:14, "Bug-Free BET Analysis", is
transcribed and analyzed in Fig. 1

    "we can write proofs in real numbers. We can't write proofs, the

    this stuff will be proofs about real numbers. And we use
    polymorphic functions. [...] we can use this to sort of bridge
    the realm of computing and the realm of proofs."

At the close of the lecture a student asks whether, since the proof
is about reals and the program uses floating point, a subset of the
reals should be used instead. The lecturer's answer, condensed from
the captions [1]:

1) No; it is harder to prove things about such a subset.
2) Floating-point numbers are a subset of the reals, and in fact of
   the rationals.
3) A function on floating-point numbers will not give the same answer
   as the function on reals; a square root has infinitely many digits
   in one and not in the other.
4) Programs must be executable to be useful. The guarantee offered is
   that the same function is used in both realms. The proofs hold for
   the reals; "those proofs don't hold" for the same function on
   floating-point numbers.

The author's statement of the topic [P01-2026-11-25-C, #007]: Lean
allows proofs using real numbers, yet not floating-point errors.

III. OPENING QUESTIONS
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Q1  Definition. Stated as a theorem, what does a proof about the real
    instance of a function guarantee about its floating-point
    instance?

Q2  Mechanism. How does one definition serve as both a real function
    to prove about and a floating-point function to run?

Q3  Conditions. What must be added for a real-number theorem to yield
    a floating-point statement, and what form does that statement
    take?

Q4  Scope. Where is the gap not small: cancellation, loss of
    associativity, comparisons and branches, iteration counts, the
    special values of S6?

Q5  Dispute. The student asks for "a subset of the reals" to compute
    with. The answer given is no. What are the candidates, and what
    does each cost?

Q6  Lens. Which engineering practice already manages an ideal design
    realized with imperfect arithmetic, and where does the comparison
    fail?

Q7  Cost. What does a proved error bound cost, compared with a proof
    over the reals alone? When is it worth it?

Q8  Implication. When a scientific result is "proved" over the reals
    and computed in floating point, what may the paper claim?

Q9  Refutation. What is the smallest function, proved correct over
    the reals, whose floating-point instance is qualitatively wrong?

------------------------------------------------------------------------
IV. PRELIMINARY ANALYSIS
------------------------------------------------------------------------

This section is the assistant's reasoning, offered for the author's
judgment. It is not yet the author's position.

A. On Q1: Three Layers

Let f be the function, f_R its instance at the reals, and f_F its
instance at floating-point numbers. Three separate claims are in
play.

  Layer 1  The theory is correct:    f_R has the stated property.
  Layer 2  The code is the theory:   f_F and f_R are one definition.
  Layer 3  The code is close:        f_F(x) is near f_R(x).

The lecture proves layer 1 and obtains layer 2 by construction.
Layer 3 is assumption S4. The answer to Q1 as the method stands is
therefore: about the floating-point values, nothing. What is
guaranteed is that no transcription error separates the formula
proved from the formula run -- which removes the semantic errors of
RT-01 (a wrong sign, transposed indices) and no numerical error.

B. On Q2 and Q9: The Mechanism, and the Smallest Counterexample

A definition written over an arbitrary type that has the needed
operations can be used at any such type:

    def f {A : Type} [Add A] [Sub A] (a b : A) : A := a + b - b

    theorem f_real (a b : Real) : f a b = a := by simp [f]

    #eval f (1.0 : Float) 1e16        -- expected output: 0.000000

Near 1e16 adjacent double-precision values are 2 apart, so 1 + 1e16
rounds back to 1e16 and the result is 0, not 1. The theorem is true,
the program is the same text, and the answer is wrong by 100
percent. This is the lecturer's own example, a + b - b, made
concrete, and it answers Q9. The real instance is for proving, not
for running; what Lean does when asked to evaluate it is to be
checked (A-01).

C. On the "Subset" Remark: A Subset, but Not a Substructure

The finite floating-point numbers F are a subset of the rationals.
But floating-point addition is not the addition of the reals
restricted to F. It is real addition followed by rounding:

        a (+) b  =  rnd(a + b)                                   (1)

F is not closed under real addition, and the operation (+) is not
associative. So no theorem about the field of reals restricts to F:
F with its own operations is not a subfield, or even a ring. This is
the precise sense in which "the same proofs" cannot be written, and
it is why "subset of the reals" is true of the values and misleading
about the arithmetic.

D. On Q3: The Form of a Layer-3 Statement

The standard model of floating-point arithmetic [3] states, for each
basic operation and barring overflow and underflow,

        fl(a op b)  =  (a op b)(1 + d),     |d| <= u             (2)

with u = 2^-53 in double precision, rounding to nearest [2]. A
layer-3 statement is then a bound

        | f_F(x) - f_R(x) |  <=  e(x)                            (3)

derived by carrying (2) through every operation of f. Statement (3)
is a theorem about real numbers -- the d's are real -- so it can be
stated and proved in Lean over the reals. What it needs is (2) as a
hypothesis about the machine, taken on trust or from a formal model
of IEEE 754 arithmetic. Such models exist for other proof assistants
[4]; what exists for Lean is not known to this note (SIDE-01).

E. On Q4: Where the Gap Is Not Small

1) Cancellation: subtracting nearly equal quantities, as in B.
2) Comparisons. If f contains "if x < y", the two instances can take
   different branches, and then (3) fails without warning: assumption
   S5. Over the reals a comparison is not even computable in general,
   so a function with a branch does not instantiate at both types
   without extra machinery. In Fig. 1 the unproved pipeline step,
   filtering to the "BET regime", is exactly such a comparison.
3) Iteration: a loop that runs "until converged" may run a different
   number of times, or not stop.
4) The special values of S6.

F. On Q5: The Candidates for "a Subset"

1) Rationals. Exact and executable; closed under the field
   operations, so the real theorems about +, -, *, / do apply. No
   square roots, and numerators and denominators grow without bound.
2) Intervals. Compute with enclosures [lo, hi] in floating point,
   rounded outward; prove once that the real result lies inside.
   Layer 3 is then carried by the data type. Cost: intervals widen.
3) Floating point with a formal model and bound (3). The full
   answer; the most proof effort.
4) The lecture's choice: floating point, layer 3 unproved.

The lecturer's "no" is a choice of 4 over 1 to 3 on grounds of cost,
which is defensible for a first course and is an engineering
decision, not a mathematical necessity.

G. On Q6: Lens for the Reader of DEC-00, Section III-A

1) Tolerance analysis. Equation (2) says each arithmetic operation is
   an ideal operation followed by a gain of (1 + d), with d inside a
   tolerance band of 1.1e-16. A computation is a network of ideal
   components each with a gain tolerance, and (3) is its worst-case
   tolerance analysis. Conditioning is sensitivity: an
   ill-conditioned problem is a high-Q network, in which a small
   component error moves the response a long way.

   Where it breaks: component tolerances are independent and random
   from unit to unit; the d's are deterministic, repeat exactly on
   every run, and are correlated with the signal. Statistical
   (root-sum-square) tolerancing therefore has no warrant here; only
   the worst case is a theorem.

2) Common-mode rejection. Computing a + b - b with b >> a is a
   differential measurement of a small signal on a large common-mode
   level: the answer is limited by the rejection ratio, here the 53
   bits of the significand. The remedy is the same in both fields:
   rearrange so that the large quantity never has to be subtracted.

3) Fixed-point filters. A filter designed as H(z) over the reals and
   built with quantized coefficients and quantized products has
   three distinct departures from the design [5]: poles moved by
   coefficient quantization (a different linear system); round-off
   noise, modeled as an additive source of variance q^2/12 at each
   multiplier and shaped by the transfer function from that node to
   the output; and limit cycles (a nonlinear effect the linear model
   cannot show). The noise model is a layer-3 statement that the
   field accepts without proof. Layers 1 and 2 correspond to "the
   schematic is right": the proof verifies the schematic, not the
   board.

   Where it breaks: the noise model is statistical and empirical;
   (3) is deterministic and proved. The limit cycle corresponds to
   the branch problem of E-2, where neither model holds.

H. Preliminary Position

The author's statement is accurate as a description of the lecture's
method and too strong as a description of Lean. Lean allows proofs
over the reals; the lecture's method proves nothing about
floating-point error; but a floating-point error bound of form (3)
is itself a real-number statement and nothing prevents its being
proved in Lean, given a model of the arithmetic. The gap is a missing
library and a cost, not a limit of the logic.
